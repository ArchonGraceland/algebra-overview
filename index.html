01Order of Operations (PEMDAS)

Why it matters: PEMDAS ensures everyone solves math problems the same way and gets the same answer. Without a standard order, the same expression could yield different results. This is critical in engineering, programming, finance, and science where consistency is essential.

Real-world use: Spreadsheet formulas, financial calculations, computer algorithms, and any collaborative work where multiple people need to interpret the same mathematical expression identically.

Parentheses → Exponents → Multiplication & Division (left to right) → Addition & Subtraction (left to right)

Example: \( 2 + 3 \times (4 - 1)^2 = 29 \)

02Negative Number Rules

Why it matters: Negative numbers represent losses, debts, temperatures below zero, and positions below reference points. Understanding sign rules is essential for correctly representing and manipulating real-world quantities that can be both positive and negative.

Real-world use: Banking (credits and debits), accounting, weather forecasting, physics (direction of forces), and any situation involving gains and losses or above/below measurements.

Sign Rules for Multiplication / Division

  • Same signs → Positive
  • Different signs → Negative
  • \( (-4) \times (-6) = 24 \)
  • \( 12 \div (-3) = -4 \)

Addition / Subtraction

\( a + (-b) = a - b \)

\( a - (-b) = a + b \)

03Properties of Real Numbers

Why it matters: These properties are the foundation of all algebra. They allow us to rearrange and simplify expressions, solve equations, and prove that different-looking expressions are actually equivalent. They're the "rules of the game" that make algebra work.

Real-world use: Software optimization (reordering operations for efficiency), circuit design (combining resistors), construction (combining materials or dimensions), and any field where you need to prove mathematical equivalence.

Commutative: \( a + b = b + a \), \( a \times b = b \times a \)
Associative: \( (a + b) + c = a + (b + c) \)
Distributive: \( a(b + c) = ab + ac \)
Identity: \( a + 0 = a \), \( a \times 1 = a \)
Inverse: \( a + (-a) = 0 \), \( a \times \frac{1}{a} = 1 \) (a ≠ 0)

04Exponent Rules

Why it matters: Exponents simplify notation and calculations for repeated multiplication. They also model exponential growth and decay in nature, making them essential for understanding population growth, radioactive decay, and compound interest.

Real-world use: Finance (compound interest calculations), biology (bacterial growth), physics (radioactive decay), computer science (data storage capacity), and any phenomenon involving exponential change.

\( a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \)
\( \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \) (a ≠ 0)
\( (a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n} \)
\( a^0 = 1 \) (a ≠ 0)
\( a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \) (a ≠ 0)
\( (ab)^n = a^n \cdot b^n \)

05Fraction Rules

Why it matters: Fractions represent parts of a whole and are more precise than decimals in many contexts. Understanding fraction operations is crucial for cooking, construction, medicine, and any work involving measurements or proportions.

Real-world use: Cooking recipes (scaling ingredients), construction (fractional measurements), medicine (dosage calculations), automotive repair (bolt sizes), and anywhere precision with partial quantities is needed.

  • Multiply: \( \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot c}{b \cdot d} \)
  • Divide: \( \frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a \cdot d}{b \cdot c} \)
  • Add/Subtract: \( \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd} \)

06Solving Equations & Inequalities

Why it matters: Equation solving is the core of algebra. It's how we find unknown values, solve real-world problems, and make predictions. Inequalities help us work with ranges and constraints, which is essential for optimization and decision-making.

Real-world use: Engineering design (finding component specifications), business (break-even analysis), budgeting (determining spending limits), physics (finding when an object reaches a certain height), and any problem requiring you to find unknown values or acceptable ranges.

Do the same operation to both sides to keep the equation balanced.

Inequality note: When multiplying or dividing by a negative number, flip the inequality sign.

Example: \( -2x > 8 \) → divide by -2 → \( x < -4 \)

07FOIL Method

Why it matters: FOIL is a systematic way to multiply binomials, which is a foundational skill for factoring, solving quadratic equations, and simplifying complex expressions. It helps prevent missing terms and ensures accurate results.

Real-world use: Expanding polynomial expressions in physics calculations, determining areas and volumes in construction, and any field that uses quadratic models (economics, engineering, ballistics).

FOIL is a mnemonic acronym that helps you remember the steps for multiplying two binomials. It stands for First, Outer, Inner, Last, representing the four products you need to create when multiplying binomial expressions.

General formula: \( (a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd \)

F – First: Multiply the first terms of each binomial → \( a \cdot c \)
O – Outer: Multiply the outer terms → \( a \cdot d \)
I – Inner: Multiply the inner terms → \( b \cdot c \)
L – Last: Multiply the last terms of each binomial → \( b \cdot d \)

Then add all four products: \( ac + ad + bc + bd \)

Worked Example: \( (x + 3)(x - 2) \)

F: \( x \cdot x = x^2 \)

O: \( x \cdot (-2) = -2x \)

I: \( 3 \cdot x = 3x \)

L: \( 3 \cdot (-2) = -6 \)

Result: \( x^2 - 2x + 3x - 6 = x^2 + x - 6 \)

08Advanced Factoring

Why it matters: Factoring is the reverse of multiplication and is essential for solving equations, simplifying complex expressions, and finding roots. Different factoring patterns appear frequently in advanced mathematics and applied fields.

Real-world use: Signal processing (polynomial analysis), economics (supply and demand curves), structural engineering (stress calculations), and cryptography (factoring large numbers).

Difference of Squares:

\( a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) \)

Sum of Cubes:

\( a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2) \)

Difference of Cubes:

\( a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2) \)

Perfect Square Trinomial:

\( a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2 = (a \pm b)^2 \)

09Radical Rules

Why it matters: Radicals represent roots and fractional exponents, which appear in geometry, physics, and engineering. They're essential for solving problems involving areas, volumes, distances, and natural phenomena.

Real-world use: Architecture (calculating diagonal measurements), physics (motion equations, kinetic energy), statistics (standard deviation), computer graphics (distance calculations), and any problem requiring square roots or higher-order roots.

\( \sqrt[n]{ab} = \sqrt[n]{a} \cdot \sqrt[n]{b} \)
\( \sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}} \) (b ≠ 0)
\( \sqrt[n]{a^m} = a^{m/n} \)
Rationalize denominator when needed

10Quadratic Formula

Why it matters: The quadratic formula is one of the most powerful tools in algebra. It solves any quadratic equation, no matter how complicated, in a systematic way. It's the key to finding roots of polynomials and solving real-world problems modeled by quadratic functions.

Real-world use: Physics (projectile motion, falling objects), engineering (beam stress, circuit design), business (profit maximization), economics (market equilibrium), and any field that uses parabolic or quadratic models.

For \( ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \):

\( x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \)

11Complex Numbers

Why it matters: Complex numbers extend the number system to solve problems that have no real solutions (like taking the square root of a negative number). They're essential in advanced mathematics and have surprising applications in the real world.

Real-world use: Electrical engineering (AC circuit analysis), quantum mechanics, signal processing, fluid dynamics, and any field involving waves, oscillations, or rotations in multi-dimensional spaces.

\( i = \sqrt{-1} \), so \( i^2 = -1 \)

Powers of \( i \) cycle every 4: \( i, -1, -i, 1 \)

Multiply using FOIL and replace \( i^2 \) with -1.

12Logarithm Properties

Why it matters: Logarithms are the inverse of exponentials and turn multiplication into addition, making complex calculations manageable. They're essential for understanding phenomena involving exponential growth and decay, and for solving exponential equations.

Real-world use: Finance (calculating compound interest periods), geology (earthquake magnitude scale), chemistry (pH calculations), astronomy (star brightness), information technology (algorithm complexity), and any field studying exponential processes.

Product: \( \log_b (xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y \)
Quotient: \( \log_b \left( \frac{x}{y} \right) = \log_b x - \log_b y \)
Power: \( \log_b (x^k) = k \log_b x \)
Inverse: \( b^{\log_b x} = x \)

13Function Transformations

Why it matters: Transformations show how changes to function parameters affect the graph. Understanding transformations allows you to quickly sketch complex functions and understand how to adjust models to fit different scenarios.

Real-world use: Data visualization and analysis, signal processing, physics (wave behavior), economics (demand curve adjustments), game development (sprite positioning), and any field that works with graphical data or modeling.

For any function \( f(x) \):

  • Up/down: \( f(x) \pm k \)
  • Left/right: \( f(x \pm h) \)
  • Reflection x-axis: \( -f(x) \)
  • Reflection y-axis: \( f(-x) \)
  • Vertical stretch: \( a \cdot f(x) \) (|a| > 1 = stretch)

14Conic Sections

Why it matters: Conic sections (circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas) describe many natural and engineered phenomena. They appear in orbital mechanics, antenna design, architecture, and countless applications across science and engineering.

Real-world use: Astronomy (planetary orbits), satellite communications (antenna design), bridge construction (parabolic arches), optical design (reflective surfaces), navigation systems (LORAN), and any system involving curved paths or surfaces.

Circle:

\( (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 \)

Parabola:

\( y = a(x - h)^2 + k \)

Ellipse:

\( \dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1 \)

Hyperbola:

\( \dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1 \)
